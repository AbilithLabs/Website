Three proposed modes

Enhance

Add precision or steadiness to something you can already do, for as long as you want it.

Guide

Help coordinate an unfamiliar movement, or physically show how it goes.

Learn

Practice with help that steps back as you take over.

Agency

You set the direction.

  1. Step 1

    Choose the action

    You decide what to make and how it should feel. The system starts from your intent, never from its own.

  2. Step 2

    Receive bounded help

    Assistance stays within limits set for you and the task, so it adds to your movement instead of taking it over.

  3. Step 3

    Change it or stop it

    Move differently and the help should follow. Stop, and it should stop with you.

These are the design principles Hermes 1.0 is being built around. The system is in early development.

Engineering approach

Sense. Adapt. Apply.

Sense

Read how the fingers, wrist and elbow are moving, how the muscles are firing, and where the hand meets an object.

Adapt

Fit assistance to the person wearing it and to what they are trying to do.

Apply

Deliver help through local feedback control, close to the actuators, so it responds within the movement.

Hermes pairs with camera smart glasses, which see the task and the object in your hands. Skills are captured from expert movement and interpreted by higher-level AI. Physical control runs locally on the device, inside the movement.

Thesis

The next interface is movement.

Everyone expects physical intelligence to arrive as robots in the home. We think it arrives on the body.

Most of what people want to make still has to pass through their hands and arms. A melody needs fingers that keep time. A plate needs a hand that holds still. A serve needs an arm that moves as one piece from shoulder to fingertip. When the body can't keep up, the idea stays in your head.

Abilith is building wearable robotics that works with your movement. A system worn from the fingertips to the bicep that can add speed, strength and precision, guide an unfamiliar action, physically show how a movement goes, and help you learn it.

Skills you can download

The best people in the world carry skills that took decades to build: a Michelin chef's knife work, a champion's serve, the hands of a master technician assembling something intricate. We want to capture that movement and make it available to anyone wearing an Abilith system. You load the skill, and the device moves with you through it.

Beyond natural ability

Some of what the device adds will go past what a human body does on its own: steadier, faster, more precise. We see that as the point. Physical ability should be something people can extend, the way software extended what we can think through and communicate.

The wearer is the author

You choose what to create and how to express it. Abilith's job is to help your body carry out that intent. Assistance stays bounded, follows your lead, and can be changed or stopped at any time.

Two ways to use it

One is learning. Help starts strong and steps back as you take over, so the skill stays with you. The other is ongoing augmentation, kept for as long as it is useful. Both are first-class.

Built on the glasses

Camera smart glasses are putting AI in front of people's eyes. They can see what you are looking at and what you are trying to do, but they cannot help you do it. Hermes is designed to pair with glasses like these as its input: the glasses supply the context, and Abilith supplies the physical ability. Glasses give AI eyes on the world. Hermes gives it hands, and they are yours.

Why cybernetics, not household robots

A robot in the home has to understand a whole house and act on its own. A wearable starts with a human who already understands the task and only needs more ability. That is a shorter path to something people use every day, and we think it will be hardware's breakout moment.

Where this stands

Research overview

Start with the hand. Explore what follows.

Start with the hand. Then the arm. Then a library of human skill.

Hermes 1.0

Hermes 1.0 runs from the fingertips to the bicep. Light guides on the finger segments route cables over the back of the hand to a drive at the wrist, leaving the fingertips and palm free for touch. A lateral strut and hinge carry the system across the elbow to a cuff at the bicep, which holds the main drive and reads muscle activity.

First: controlled finger assistance

The first experiment applies small, limited assistance to a single finger movement. We want to learn how it feels, whether the wearer can override it, and how well the device fits different hands.

Then: capture and playback

Next, we record an expert's movement and play it back through the device, across two different tasks on the same hardware. That shows which parts of the approach carry over between skills.

Next: the full arm and a skill library

With the wrist and elbow in the loop, larger movements become possible: a serve, a knife stroke, a long reach on an assembly line. Each captured skill joins a library that grows across cooking, sport, music and manufacturing.

Questions we are answering

  • Capture. How do you record an expert's skill so it transfers to a different body?
  • Control. How does a person direct the help, redirect it mid-movement, or stop it?
  • Comfort. Can the device be worn through a full session without getting in the way?
  • Retention. When assistance is reduced or removed, what stays with you?

System structure

Camera smart glasses supply context: the task, the object and where your hands are. Sensing from the fingertips to the bicep reads movement, contact and muscle activity. An adaptation layer tunes assistance to the wearer, the task and the skill being played back. A local feedback controller on the device applies it. Higher-level AI interprets skills and goals, while physical control stays local.

Status

Hermes 1.0 is in early development. There are no study results yet, and the device shown on this site is a rendered concept. A white paper is in preparation.
